Context : 
The building sector accounts for a substantial share of national carbon emissions, both through the production of materials and through the use and end-of-life of buildings, creating growing pressure to decarbonize construction materials. In France, the RE2020 environmental regulation (2022) now imposes a minimum share of bio-based materials in new buildings, steering the timber-construction sector toward alternatives such as straw insulation. A short-chain, locally sourced agricultural by-product, straw has a low embodied carbon footprint and is positioned as a low-carbon substitute for conventional insulation materials, foremost among them glass wool, a mineral insulation manufactured industrially at large scale, whose mature and standardized supply chain guarantees well-characterized performance. This dynamic is accompanied by the growth of the straw-bale construction sector, notably driven by the French Straw Construction Network, with rising demand for straw as an insulation material in timber-frame buildings. The usual method for comparing these two materials remains Life Cycle Assessment, an inventory-based approach built on carbon footprint per square meter, thermal resistance, and unit cost, on which straw generally scores better than glass wool. This accounting-based approach nonetheless has limits: it does not capture what would happen if demand for straw increased sharply, nor the structural and logistical trade-offs involved in substituting glass wool with straw, nor, more broadly, the feedback dynamics inherent to the timber-panel production system as a whole. This observation motivates the use of a systemic approach, based on the Causal Loop Diagram (CLD), to model how the choice of insulation material propagates through cost, availability, structural design, and logistics.

Method : 
the causal loop diagram. A causal link connects two variables A and B and carries a polarity that answers the following question: if A increases, all else being equal, what happens to B? A positive link indicates that the two variables move in the same direction, while a negative link indicates that they move in opposite directions. This is not an observed correlation but a hypothesis of causal influence put forward by the modeler, whose validity should be checked against the literature, expert opinion, or the so-called “Norwegian laughing test,” rather than taken for granted; a link may also carry a delay, meaning the effect on the target variable only manifests after some time, which strongly influences the system's dynamic behavior, in particular the emergence of oscillations. A loop refers to a closed chain of causal links connecting a variable back to itself; it is this structure, rather than any single variable, that explains the system's behavior over time. A loop is said to be reinforcing when an initial change, after a full pass around the loop, comes back to amplify that same change in the same direction, producing dynamics of exponential growth or of a vicious or virtuous circle; it is characterized by an even number of negative links along the loop's path. Conversely, a loop is said to be balancing when an initial change, after a full pass around the loop, comes back to counteract that same change, producing stabilizing dynamics around a target or a constraint; it is characterized by an odd number of negative links. As an illustration, the loop linking material attractiveness to volume sold, to availability, to price, to production cost, and then to cumulative cost, before closing back on attractiveness, contains three negative links: it is therefore a balancing loop, in which a rise in a material's attractiveness eventually, after a full pass around the loop, turns back against itself through the effect of scarcity and price.

The CLD Studio tool :
To operationalize this approach, an interactive causal-modeling tool was used, built as a standalone web application that runs entirely in the browser. Its distinguishing feature compared with common diagramming tools such as drawio, Miro, or Kumu, which remain purely qualitative, lies in the ability to replace a simple arrow-based link with a calculation or a formula connected to real data, without resorting to the heavier formalism of system-dynamics tools such as Vensim or Stella. Each variable can thus be linked to an external data table, its value being computed by looking up that table for a selected row, while an iteration engine recalculates the entire model step by step, automatically converting link delays into calculation steps, which makes it possible to observe the system's trajectory over time rather than merely a qualitative direction of change. Changing an assumption, for example choosing an alternative material, then only requires selecting a different row in the data table, with all connected variables recalculated instantly. The tool also automatically detects every loop in the graph and classifies it as reinforcing or balancing based on the parity of the number of negative links, with no manual intervention from the user, and allows particularly relevant loops to be highlighted and their causal neighborhood explored through successive proximity levels. A built-in interactive guide walks users through all of these features. It should be emphasized that no domain-specific logic is hard-coded into the tool, which is still under active development (work in progress): the dataset relating to timber construction and insulation materials is only one example application among many, and the tool can be used to model any system exhibiting feedback effects, whether in public health, energy, agriculture, circular economy, or supply chains, as long as a corresponding set of variables and data is provided.

Application to the case study :
Four loops were identified as particularly significant for capturing the systemic impact of replacing glass wool with straw, beyond the material's carbon footprint alone. The first, a balancing loop, links material attractiveness to annual volume sold, to insulation availability, to unit price, and then to production and cumulative costs, before closing back on initial attractiveness: it reflects the fact that growing enthusiasm for straw, a material with an inherently constrained agricultural and local supply chain, tends to push up its price and associated costs, which eventually erodes its attractiveness, unlike glass wool, whose industrial supply chain is not subject to this scarcity constraint. The second, a reinforcing loop, links transport frequency to on-site storage and then to installation rate, before closing back on transport frequency; specific to on-site logistics organization rather than to the material itself, it could nonetheless saturate faster with straw, which is bulkier to store in bales than glass wool packaged in compact rolls. The third, a balancing loop, links stud spacing to stud linear length and then to stud surface area, then to the required insulation thickness, before closing back on spacing: it reflects the structural and thermal trade-off specific to straw, whose higher thermal conductivity compared with glass wool requires a greater wall thickness for an equivalent thermal resistance, thereby increasing the amount of timber framing required and the associated thermal bridging. The fourth, finally, links the mass of a timber-frame panel to the number of panels that can be transported per truck: since straw is heavier or bulkier per useful cubic meter than glass wool at equivalent insulation performance, fewer panels can be loaded per truck, increasing the number of trips required and potentially offsetting, through a rebound effect on transport, part of the expected carbon benefit of the bio-based material. Together, these four loops illustrate how a material choice motivated by a favorable carbon footprint can nonetheless generate, once placed back into the production system it belongs to, technical, logistical, and economic constraints likely to limit its deployment at scale.
